Accountability in the courts

ProPublica

In a yearlong investigation into Pennsylvania's post-conviction system, ProPublica found that low pay, heavy caseloads and lax rules let appointed lawyers fall years behind and routinely file "Finley letters", no-merit briefs arguing against their own clients.

In a yearlong investigation into Pennsylvania's post-conviction system, ProPublica found that low pay, heavy caseloads and lax rules let appointed lawyers fall years behind and routinely file "Finley letters", no-merit briefs arguing against their own clients. Three-quarters of the time, Philadelphia lawyers who filed one had not spoken with the client, contacted the trial lawyer or obtained police files. Judges approved nearly all such letters, some with obvious errors or the wrong client named, and kept assigning cases to lawyers the appeals court had criticised. Over 14 years the Superior Court flagged more than 250 cases where judges let ineffective representation stand and more than 200 where lawyers abandoned clients; more than 100 cases bounced back and forth repeatedly. Only six lawyers in a decade have been publicly sanctioned for this work, and no lower-court judge has been publicly disciplined.
