Two critical vulnerabilities in PHP, the programming language behind almost 70% of websites, made governments and companies take sites offline on Wednesday while they rushed to patch.

Two critical vulnerabilities in PHP, the programming language behind almost 70% of websites, made governments and companies take sites offline on Wednesday while they rushed to patch. The flaws, nicknamed PHP2Shell, let an attacker run commands on a server remotely without a password or account. The US National Security Agency issued a rare public warning telling anyone who could not patch immediately to take PHP systems offline, and Britain's National Cyber Security Centre told organisations to check for backdoors. Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Finland and the Czech Republic shut government sites. In the UK, the Edinburgh, Bristol and Oxford council sites went down, as did the Paris police prefecture's site and the Dutch digital-identity service. Wikimedia took some systems down as a precaution, and Japan's prime minister Sanae Takaichi warned about the risk on social media. AI tools found the bugs. The security firm Calif, working with OpenAI, reported the first flaw in August. Wiz, owned by Alphabet, reported the second and says they rank among the most severe flaws "arguably in software history": they had been in the code for more than a decade and would have been extremely hard for a human to exploit. The PHP Foundation, which is run by volunteers, has released patches. Experts warn of a "race to exploit": AI can reverse-engineer a public fix quickly, and poorly maintained sites may stay exposed for months. Amazon says someone tried the exploit on one of its honeypots on 17 October, four days before disclosure, and the White House says it was told on Friday. The Economist says two more separate flaws were published by Thursday, including one in GitLab rated 10 out of 10 for severity. It argues that announcing bugs before fixes were ready, to give defenders a head start, also tipped off attackers.

Barclays will cut about 5,000 of its 93,000 staff as part of plans to save more than £1.1bn in 2027. About 1,000 have already gone this year. Meta's $25bn privacy verdict and a mixed night for Big Tech earnings

Al Jazeera · The Guardian · WSJ What's News

Judge Francis Mathew of the New Mexico state court ordered Meta to pay $25bn after a Santa Fe jury found on 25 September that 26 of 29 statements by the company and its leaders on privacy and content moderation were misleading.

Judge Francis Mathew of the New Mexico state court ordered Meta to pay $25bn after a Santa Fe jury found on 25 September that 26 of 29 statements by the company and its leaders on privacy and content moderation were misleading. The jury counted more than 43m violations of the state's consumer-protection law. The judge said a penalty of more than $219bn could be justified under state law, which allows up to $5,000 per violation, but cut it to $25bn. The state had asked for $35–40bn and Meta for a cap of $3.45bn. He wrote that Facebook's wrongdoing went "to the heart" of its core business. The case dates from 2021 and grew out of Cambridge Analytica's collection of data from up to 87m users. Attorney general Raúl Torrez called it one of the largest state verdicts against a company in US history and said the money goes to New Mexico schools. Meta's chief legal officer CJ Mahoney said the company will appeal and that the ruling makes it "all but impossible to do business" in the state. The penalty dwarfs the FTC's $5bn fine in 2019. It is New Mexico's second win against Meta this year, after a total of $942m in a teen-safety case.

France's 10-year yield rose nine basis points to 5.089% early on Thursday, the highest since July 2022, after passing 5% on Wednesday for the first time since 2002. The IIF says global debt rose $10tn in the first half to more than $365tn, and advanced economies paid over $3.3tn in bond interest last year, more than world defence spending. The WSJ says France relies unusually heavily on foreign investors who have little reason to rescue it.

SCOTUSblog · The Guardian · WSJ Politics

In a one-sentence unsigned order on Wednesday, the court refused the Trump administration's request to pause Judge Royce Lamberth's 26 August order.

In a one-sentence unsigned order on Wednesday, the court refused the Trump administration's request to pause Judge Royce Lamberth's 26 August order. That order blocks the Federal Bureau of Prisons' February 2026 policy ending hormone therapy, transition surgery and "social accommodations" such as clothing, wigs and hair-removal devices; psychiatric care would have continued. Three transgender inmates, held in New Jersey, Florida and Minnesota, brought the class action. They say federal prisons have provided hormone treatment since at least 1992 and that withdrawing it risks depression, self-harm and suicide. The Guardian calls it an unusual win for transgender rights at a court that has upheld the military ban, school sports limits and the passport policy. NPR counted more than 2,000 transgender people in federal custody in 2025.

Solicitor General Sauer has asked the court to pause, and summarily reverse, Judge Kathleen Cardone's 2 October order in El Paso. SCOTUSblog

In DHS v.

In DHS v. D.V.D., the court will review the administration's policy of deporting migrants to any country that will take them. Under the policy, a country that gives diplomatic assurances against persecution needs no further process. If a country gives no assurance, migrants get "generally 24 hours" to raise fears. Judge Brian Murphy in Boston vacated the policy in February. He found that it breaches the Immigration and Nationality Act's protection against persecution, and that DHS had defied his orders, including by sending a Guatemalan man to Mexico, from where he was returned to Guatemala. The 1st Circuit largely agreed in September. News reports say DHS has tried to send people to South Sudan, Cameroon, the Central African Republic and about three dozen other countries, and more than 20,000 non-Mexicans to Mexico. Law professor César García Hernández expects the justices to decide the technical question of whether a district-court class action can hear such claims at all, as the solicitor general argues it cannot, rather than the risks migrants face.
