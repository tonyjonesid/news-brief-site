Al Jazeera · Economist The World in Brief · WSJ Politics

After an eight-month probe into what Washington calls "structural excess capacity" (subsidies, state-suppressed wages and state-owned firms in industries from steel and cars to chips and solar panels), the US Trade Representative on Wednesday proposed duties on China, the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Thailand, Bangladesh and Cambodia; Mexico and Norway were investigated but left out for now because their reviews continue.

After an eight-month probe into what Washington calls "structural excess capacity" (subsidies, state-suppressed wages and state-owned firms in industries from steel and cars to chips and solar panels), the US Trade Representative on Wednesday proposed duties on China, the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Thailand, Bangladesh and Cambodia; Mexico and Norway were investigated but left out for now because their reviews continue. A senior official told Reuters, as quoted by Al Jazeera, that China would face a proposed 20% levy on top of roughly 25% left over from Trump's first term, that Vietnam also faces 20%, that the new duties would not stack on the July forced-labour tariffs and would respect caps in existing deals (15% for the EU, Japan and South Korea, 19% for Malaysia), and that about $30bn of Chinese goods under review for cuts would be exempt. It is the latest attempt to rebuild the tariff wall the Supreme Court struck down in February. Trade expert Dmitry Grozoubinski called these the most legally robust of Trump's tariffs so far but warned that shifting rates deter factory investment, while Georgetown's Gregory Shaffer called the use of Section 301 cynical. China's foreign ministry dismissed "overcapacity" as a false issue, the EU rejected the premise that Europe is a source of it, Switzerland rejected the findings and Norway welcomed its exclusion. The White House separately proposed a 10% tariff on finished Embraer jets and higher over-quota duties on Brazilian sugar. The Nasdaq closed 0.6% lower after falling more than 4% early, gold dropped over 3% to a one-year low, and the US 10-year yield fell 6.8 basis points to 4.834%. The Economist and WSJ Politics had flagged the announcement in advance.

The Century Foundation estimates a typical Thanksgiving meal costs 11% more than last year, with turkey up 26% and green beans up almost 37%, while the American Farm Bureau puts the rise for a 10-person meal at 6%, the first increase since 2022. Supreme Court: the December sitting opens Monday

SCOTUSblog

On November 30 the justices hear Crowther v.

On November 30 the justices hear Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, brought by a former Augusta University art professor and Georgia Tech's former women's basketball coach, whose sex-discrimination and retaliation claims the 11th Circuit threw out on the ground that Title IX gives employees no right to sue. The employees point to Title IX's broad text, its lack of an employment exception and precedents such as North Haven v. Bell; the university system argues that Congress, weeks earlier, had given school employees Title VII's detailed remedy with its EEOC filing requirement and damages caps, so it would not have silently created a second one, and that Spending Clause rules mean a state must clearly agree to private suits. West Virginia and 20 states back the university, the Trump administration urges a ruling on statutory grounds alone, and the National Education Association warns that losing the remedy would hurt an already strained teaching workforce. SCOTUSblog notes the bench has grown reluctant to allow private suits without clear statutory authorisation.

SCOTUSblog

Also on Monday, Wassily v.

Also on Monday, Wassily v. Blanche asks whether two men whose asylum was terminated after criminal convictions (an Egyptian granted asylum in 2000 and a Guatemalan granted it in 1996) can still apply for permanent residence under 8 U.S.C. § 1159(b), which covers "any alien granted asylum". A divided 2nd Circuit panel read the phrase as requiring current asylum status, as has the 4th Circuit, while the 5th Circuit disagrees. The men argue that Congress spelled out continuing conditions elsewhere, that a categorical bar would gut officials' humanitarian waiver discretion, and that the rule of lenity applies; Solicitor General D. John Sauer says the statute describes moving from one current status to another and that lenity is irrelevant because the provision does not govern removal.
