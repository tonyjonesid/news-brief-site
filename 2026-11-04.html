Democrats took control of the Senate and, by the Associated Press's call, the House on Tuesday, winning in states the party had not carried in decades. By the Guardian's count they had won 54 Senate seats to the Republicans' 42, with Alaska, where Mary Peltola is challenging Dan Sullivan, still counting and possibly days from a result. Pickups included North Carolina (Roy Cooper beat former Republican national chair Michael Whatley), Ohio (Sherrod Brown ousted Jon Husted), Maine (Troy Jackson unseated Susan Collins, who conceded just before 1am), Kansas (pastor Adam Hamilton beat Roger Marshall, the state's first Democratic senator since 1932), South Carolina (paediatrician Annie Andrews beat Darline Graham, whom Trump had backed after her brother Lindsey Graham's death) and Texas (James Talarico beat Ken Paxton). In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed beat Mike Rogers to become the first Muslim US senator after a campaign the Guardian describes as largely Islamophobic. Republicans failed to take any of the Democratic seats they targeted, including Georgia, where Jon Ossoff won comfortably, and even their wins were narrow: Ashley Moody held Florida by about three points and Julia Letlow took Louisiana by about two, in a state Trump won by roughly 22. The Guardian attributes the swing to the Iran war, high petrol prices, unpopular immigration raids and Trump's primary choices. Trump posted that he "wasn't on the Ballot". Hakeem Jeffries is set to become the first Black House speaker, and Chuck Schumer told voters "relief is on the way". The WSJ's Politics newsletter adds that Democrats took Republican House seats in California, Utah, South Carolina, North Carolina, Missouri, Pennsylvania, Kentucky, New Jersey and Ohio, that the new caucus is younger and ranges from the Democratic Socialists to centrists, and that Trump is now at his weakest since 6 January 2021. On election day, the WSJ's What's News said a large national survey found high fuel and food prices were voters' top concern, with many blaming Trump.

After two hours of argument in St Mary Catholic Parish v Roy, a majority seemed ready to hold that Colorado's exclusion of Catholic preschools, which will not sign a nondiscrimination pledge requiring them to admit LGBTQ families, must face strict scrutiny. SCOTUSblog

On 9 November the court hears Nielsen v Watanabe, a federal prisoner's claim that a staff nurse denied him care for a broken tailbone, and the nurse is asking it to overrule Bivens outright.

On 9 November the court hears Nielsen v Watanabe, a federal prisoner's claim that a staff nurse denied him care for a broken tailbone, and the nurse is asking it to overrule Bivens outright. Erwin Chemerinsky explains in a SCOTUSblog column that Bivens, from 1971, is the only route to money damages against federal officers for constitutional violations, because the statute covering state officers (Section 1983) does not reach them. The court has not upheld a Bivens claim since 1980, and in Egbert v Boule (2022) it called creating one a "disfavored judicial activity". Watanabe argues that his case is the same as Carlson v Green (1980), a prison-medical-care case. The court could affirm, distinguish Carlson, overrule Carlson, or abolish Bivens. Chemerinsky argues that without it people harmed by federal agents, including ICE officers, would often have no remedy at all.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre said British food suppliers had been hit after the attacks that heated supermarket freezers across Europe, although no UK grocery chain had been affected and the impact was "minimal". Some of the more than 300 members of the British Association of Independent Meat Suppliers were caught up; one firm's cold units reached 100C, but they were empty and no meat was lost. Cyber firm Lupovis recorded 256 attempts on decoy Danfoss fridge controllers from 91 sources between 20 and 30 October, mostly using factory-default credentials, through hosting it has previously linked to Russian operations.
