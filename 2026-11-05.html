Democrats will hold at least 54 Senate seats when the new Congress opens on 3 January, and two races remain open. Supreme Court: Alabama's map again, suing federal officers and pipeline compensation

SCOTUSblog

Three newly relisted appeals (Allen v Milligan, Allen v Singleton and Allen v Caster) ask the justices to overturn a district court's renewed finding that Alabama's 2023 map, with a single Black-opportunity district, violates Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act and was intentionally discriminatory.

Three newly relisted appeals (Allen v Milligan, Allen v Singleton and Allen v Caster) ask the justices to overturn a district court's renewed finding that Alabama's 2023 map, with a single Black-opportunity district, violates Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act and was intentionally discriminatory. The lower court reached that conclusion again after this spring's Louisiana v Callais ruling changed the Section 2 standard. The Supreme Court stayed that injunction on 2 June, so the map was used in this week's elections. Alabama says the court ignored its legitimate priorities, such as keeping Mobile and Baldwin counties together, and mistook politics for race. The challengers say the case is moot because the injunction covered only 2026, and point to the removal of Selma from District 2. Also relisted is Brown v United States, which asks whether every car counts as an instrumentality of interstate commerce, so that a kidnapping entirely within Alabama can be prosecuted federally. Brown cites a split with the 10th Circuit; the solicitor general says no split exists. SCOTUSblog's daily notes say the justices confer on Friday, with orders expected on Monday, and that the administration's request to resume border-wall building in Big Bend is fully briefed and could be decided at any time.

The court hears Nielsen v Watanabe on 9 November.

The court hears Nielsen v Watanabe on 9 November. Kekai Watanabe says a federal prison paramedic in Hawaii gave him only over-the-counter painkillers after a gang fight, though he reported pain of 10 out of 10. He then spent more than two months in solitary without seeing a specialist, and an X-ray seven months later showed a fractured coccyx. The 9th Circuit let his Eighth Amendment claim go forward as indistinguishable from Carlson v Green, one of only three cases in which the court has allowed Bivens damages claims. The paramedic, backed by the Trump administration, argues that the claim arises in a new context, because the Carlson inmate died within hours, and that the prison grievance system and the Prison Litigation Reform Act give reasons to leave remedies to Congress. He also asks the court to overrule Bivens outright, building on Justice Thomas's 2022 statement in Egbert v Boule that the court would not recognise such claims today.

In Hoffmann v WBI Energy Transmission, argued next week, a North Dakota family whose land a gas pipeline would cross argues that when Congress delegates eminent domain to a private company under the Natural Gas Act, the law of the state where the land lies should set compensation.

In Hoffmann v WBI Energy Transmission, argued next week, a North Dakota family whose land a gas pipeline would cross argues that when Congress delegates eminent domain to a private company under the Natural Gas Act, the law of the state where the land lies should set compensation. North Dakota law may require the condemnor to pay the owner's reasonable attorney's fees. WBI and the federal government say only federal "just compensation" applies. They point to the 1946 General Bridge Act, which expressly adopted state law where the Natural Gas Act does not, and to United States v Miller. They also warn that the family's rule would let states discriminate against federally backed projects. Columbia's Ronald Mann expects the court's reluctance to award fees without a clear statement from Congress to weigh against the landowners.
