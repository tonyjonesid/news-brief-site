Supreme Court: the new term opens with climate suits, detention and asylum

SCOTUSblog

Opening the 2026-27 term, the court heard nearly two hours of argument in Suncor Energy v.

Opening the 2026-27 term, the court heard nearly two hours of argument in Suncor Energy v. County Commissioners of Boulder County, where the city and county seek state-law damages from two energy companies for climate costs. With Justice Alito recused, a 4-4 split would leave the Colorado Supreme Court's ruling allowing the case to go forward in place. Kannon Shanmugam, for the companies, called it an "unprecedented effort to use state law to regulate global conduct"; Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Kagan seemed unpersuaded, with Kagan asking where the Constitution or precedent supports the claim that federal law displaces such suits and likening them to the 1990s tobacco cases. Justice Kavanaugh was the companies' strongest ally, citing precedents that interstate air and water pollution are federal matters, while Justices Thomas and Barrett probed how far Boulder's theory could stretch, including to fuel retailers or a state capping emissions nationwide. Boulder's lawyer Kevin Russell said the suit only asks the companies to internalise part of the cost of their activities. A jurisdictional off-ramp the court had asked the parties to brief drew interest mainly from Justices Sotomayor and Jackson.

From its September 28 "long conference" the court granted review in Rhoney v. SCOTUSblog

In Wassily v.

In Wassily v. Blanche the court will decide whether migrants granted asylum must still hold it to adjust to permanent residence. The Justice Department reads the Immigration and Nationality Act as authorising adjustment only for someone who "currently has asylum status"; Tamer Wassily and Byron Velásquez Arreaga, who lost asylum after convictions for stalking and child endangerment and for aggravated drunk driving, argue the Refugee Act of 1980 lists its criteria in full and contains no continuing-status requirement. The text, which refers to "any alien granted asylum" in the past tense, has not changed since President Carter signed it, while Congress spent the late 1980s and 1990s steadily raising the immigration consequences of criminal convictions. The piece's author, immigration scholar César Cuauhtémoc García Hernández, frames the case as a choice between enforcing old text as written or reading it through the current political climate. The court will also hear argument Wednesday in Department of the Air Force v. Prutehi Guåhan on whether the Air Force must prepare a NEPA environmental impact statement before renewing a hazardous-waste permit to detonate munitions on a Guam beach.

In a report published Wednesday, the EU police agency's cybercrime centre said a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could derive private keys from exposed public keys and spend the funds, while the hash functions securing bitcoin's history and mining are far more resistant; "cryptocurrencies will not collapse due to quantum computing," it said, and no such machine exists yet. Roughly 6.9m BTC sit in addresses with exposed public keys, including Satoshi-era outputs that cannot be made safe retroactively, feeding the debate over whether to freeze them. Migrating every bitcoin UTXO to a quantum-resistant format would consume at least 76 days of cumulative block space, or about 300 days if a quarter of each block were reserved, and post-quantum signatures are 10 to 120 times larger than today's ECDSA signatures. Researchers increasingly treat 2029 as the deadline for credible migration plans; Europol called for wallet upgrades and coordination among developers, miners, exchanges and users now.

Pike, 50, is awake and speaking in a Nashville hospital a week after two doses of pentobarbital failed to kill her, the first known survivor of a completed US lethal-injection attempt. Still shackled to her bed with severe arm damage, she faces a long recovery. Her lawyers went to Davidson County Chancery Court on Wednesday for an order preserving drug lot numbers, needles and logs, arguing the IV lines were misplaced so the caustic drug pooled in her tissue, a risk they had flagged months earlier because of her small veins and a clotting disorder, which the Tennessee Supreme Court dismissed as "speculative". Her team wants the sentence commuted to life without parole and says a second attempt would be cruel and unusual and could breach double jeopardy. ProPublica notes this was Tennessee's third botched execution in 14 months and that ageing prisoners, questionable execution-team competence and secrecy over drug sourcing make repeats likely; prisoners facing execution in Texas and Florida this month are citing Pike's case.

Dozens of suits against OpenAI, over a teenager's suicide, an attempted overdose, a murder-suicide and two school shootings, rely on product liability, claiming ChatGPT is defectively designed.

Dozens of suits against OpenAI, over a teenager's suicide, an attempted overdose, a murder-suicide and two school shootings, rely on product liability, claiming ChatGPT is defectively designed. In a September 25 motion to dismiss a Florida case, OpenAI's Wilson Sonsini lawyers argue "information and ideas are not products", that ChatGPT is an interactive service, and that the First Amendment bars holding information providers liable for acts they inspire. Plaintiffs' counsel say an LLM has no human speaker. If courts treat chatbots as products, the test is safe design and warnings; as services, plaintiffs must prove a duty to protect users from foreseeable harm. About two dozen cases are consolidated in San Francisco Superior Court with OpenAI's response due October 30, and one plaintiffs' firm says it has 30 more in the pipeline. A judge rejected the free-speech defence in a comparable Character.AI case last year before it settled.
