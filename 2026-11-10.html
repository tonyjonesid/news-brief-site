Nvidia's Huang affair: a market hit and a new fight over chips for China

Al Jazeera · The Guardian

Huang has been Silicon Valley's loudest voice against limits on advanced-chip sales to China, and Al Jazeera reports that lawmakers moved quickly: Senators Elizabeth Warren and Jim Banks urged Congress to pass their AI Overwatch Act, which would let lawmakers scrutinise and potentially halt such exports, and Representative Bill Foster pressed his Chip Security Act, co-sponsored with Bill Huizenga, to track where exported chips end up.

Huang has been Silicon Valley's loudest voice against limits on advanced-chip sales to China, and Al Jazeera reports that lawmakers moved quickly: Senators Elizabeth Warren and Jim Banks urged Congress to pass their AI Overwatch Act, which would let lawmakers scrutinise and potentially halt such exports, and Representative Bill Foster pressed his Chip Security Act, co-sponsored with Bill Huizenga, to track where exported chips end up. Analysts said the restriction camp now has a much stronger political argument whatever the inquiry finds, while one adviser urged scepticism about anonymous leaks and warned that closing the remote-access loophole, through which Chinese firms use Nvidia-powered data centres in Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand, could be read in Beijing as breaching the trade truce. The Guardian traces the lobbying record: Trump has met Huang at least seven times this term, four times touching on export controls; he let Nvidia resume sales of previously banned chips for 15% of revenue and later the more powerful H200 for 25%, while refusing the top-end Blackwell. The pair were filmed together in Taipei at the height of that push, and the New York Times reported that Nvidia appeared to have leaned on the commerce department, via a Trump appointee, to halt a chip-smuggling inquiry into Megaspeed. Epoch AI estimates chips equal to 1.6 million H100s reached China in breach of the rules up to the end of 2025.

Supreme Court: the Big Bend wall goes ahead and a long order list

SCOTUSblog · The Guardian

In an unsigned opinion on Monday the court paused District Judge Kathleen Cardone's October order that had halted construction across the Big Bend region, including parts of the national park; only Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson would have refused.

In an unsigned opinion on Monday the court paused District Judge Kathleen Cardone's October order that had halted construction across the Big Bend region, including parts of the national park; only Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson would have refused. Cardone had found the challengers (an environmental group, a river guide and a church-preservation group) likely to win on the major-questions doctrine, reasoning that a sector covering over 25% of the border but only 0.3%–2.2% of apprehensions could not be an "area of high illegal entry" justifying the homeland security secretary's waivers of environmental law. The justices declined the government's request to reverse her outright but said it is "exceedingly likely" to win on its argument that she lacked power to hear the claim, because a major-questions challenge is statutory, not constitutional: "There is no such thing as a 'constitutional' major-questions claim." They also cited unrecoverable costs of nearly half a million dollars a day and said the government's interest outweighs the aesthetic and environmental harms alleged. The Guardian reports plans for more than 300 miles of vehicle barriers and roads, five CBP contracts worth over $6bn in the sector, and construction at about 12 miles a week; the Center for Biological Diversity called the ruling "horrific".
