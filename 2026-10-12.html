Robert Armstrong opens by noting that Pimco's chief investment officer told the FT the 10-year Treasury yield could reach 6 per cent, which he calls high but plausible if growth holds and more hedge funds sell; he adds that the yield has already climbed from 1.5 to 5.2 per cent in five years, so the market is probably nearer the top than the bottom. His main argument is that the economy is not nearly as dependent on AI as the stock market is. The share of sectors growing in output and employment is in line with its 20-year average, consumer spending supplied more than all of second-quarter GDP growth, and AI data-centre construction, at an $85bn annual rate, equals roughly a quarter of one per cent of GDP. A Fed estimate puts the wider AI boom's contribution near 0.6 percentage points of growth a year. The real channel, he argues, is the wealth effect: household equity holdings have grown by about $34tn since late 2022, and one central-bank model attributes around 40 per cent of 2025 consumption growth to it, so a bursting of the AI bubble could hurt consumption even though AI investment itself is not the engine.

Treasury sell-off spreads into corporate credit

FT Asset Management

The FT Asset Management newsletter reports that the bond sell-off is hitting corporate America.

The FT Asset Management newsletter reports that the bond sell-off is hitting corporate America. Yields on the lowest-rated companies reached 17 per cent this month, the highest since May 2020, and the risk premium on triple-C and below widened to 12 percentage points, the most since 2022, after the 10-year Treasury yield hit its highest level since 2002. Analysts quoted say floating-rate borrowers and those refinancing soon are most exposed, and Bank of America has cut its forecast for October dollar investment-grade issuance to $110bn from about $160bn. Paramount Skydance's $52bn bond sale, raised for its $111bn Warner Bros Discovery deal, saw two-year demand almost double that for 30-year paper, leaving investors with more long-dated debt than expected and prompting a sell-off after launch. The newsletter's weekly roundup adds that Pimco warned benchmark US borrowing costs could hit 6 per cent, that a French bond sell-off has drawn bargain hunters into Italian and corporate debt, and that Arini Capital's flagship fund is down almost 16 per cent this year.

Hedge-fund lending and asset-management growth

FT Asset Management

According to the newsletter, lending to hedge funds and trading firms has grown from a back-office function into a major bank profit line, with revenues from equity and fixed-income prime broking forecast by Coalition Greenwich to rise 38 per cent this year to $47.9bn.

According to the newsletter, lending to hedge funds and trading firms has grown from a back-office function into a major bank profit line, with revenues from equity and fixed-income prime broking forecast by Coalition Greenwich to rise 38 per cent this year to $47.9bn. JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs dominate. Big clients such as Citadel and Jane Street have won better terms, with the notice a bank must give before withdrawing financing stretching from days to six months for the most coveted clients. Regulators are growing uneasy about banks' rising exposure, with one saying the business has "just grown and grown". The same issue says clients of Goldman Sachs' wealth arm and of Man Group were among those hit by a data breach at EY, and that JPMorgan Asset Management aims to double its European assets to $2tn within five years, led by active ETFs, alternatives and wealth-manager partnerships, while preferring organic growth to acquisitions.

Burnham's Downing Street: Abu Dhabi, Manchester and policy

FT Inside Politics

Inside Politics argues that Andy Burnham, now prime minister, made an unforced error by saying he would be "really concerned" if Abu Dhabi sold Manchester City after the Premier League's findings on the club's finances.

Inside Politics argues that Andy Burnham, now prime minister, made an unforced error by saying he would be "really concerned" if Abu Dhabi sold Manchester City after the Premier League's findings on the club's finances. The author notes the council's housebuilding joint venture with the club's owners lapsed at the end of last year and the last planned site is already under construction, so there is little for Abu Dhabi to withdraw, and that the real exposure to any pull-back of UAE money lies in places like the Oxford-Cambridge corridor. The newsletter also points out that Burnham chaired the combined authority when it approved loans from a housing investment fund to Abu Dhabi-backed developments, that courts have twice dismissed claims of preferential treatment, and that nobody has so far shown rules were broken. It warns that Manchester's reputation risks becoming collateral damage of "Manchesterism". Its top stories add that the IFS called Labour's Budget a "work of fiction" unless a £35bn gap is explained, that Burnham will host business executives worried about tax rises, and that ministers are weighing compulsory safety reporting for frontier AI.
