The listing did not stop a global sell-off in AI-linked shares on Friday. Nvidia, chips to China and Washington's tech security

Al Jazeera

The House Foreign Affairs Committee released export-licensing data showing that the Commerce Department approved exports of 879,000 advanced Nvidia and AMD chips to Chinese companies between December 2025 and May 2026.

The House Foreign Affairs Committee released export-licensing data showing that the Commerce Department approved exports of 879,000 advanced Nvidia and AMD chips to Chinese companies between December 2025 and May 2026. Companies had asked to ship 3.4 million. The approvals included 150,000 for Tencent, 75,000 for Alibaba, 43,000 for Baidu and 4,000 for BYD, all on the Pentagon's list of alleged Chinese military-linked companies. Committee chair Brian Mast used powers under the Export Control Reform Act to make the data public. It is not clear how many chips, which included Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X, were actually shipped. A former US official called it astonishing that more than 30% went to designated military companies. Nvidia said the licences carried strict conditions and that it has since stopped shipping H200s to China. The release comes amid scrutiny of the Trump administration's December decision to allow H200 sales, after reports of former Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang's affair with an alleged Chinese spy.

World court finds Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya

The Economist World in Brief · The Guardian

The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya during its 2016-17 military campaign.

The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya during its 2016-17 military campaign. It is the first time the court has found a state responsible for the crime. Court president Yuji Iwasawa said a majority of nine of the 15 judges were convinced the acts were carried out with intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya population. The judges rejected Myanmar's claim that it was running counter-terrorism operations and found that sexual violence and limits such as a two-child rule were used to stop the group having children. The court said Myanmar's forces killed about 10,000 Rohingya. The campaign drove more than 700,000 people into Bangladesh, where about 1.2 million Rohingya now live in camps. The case was brought by the Gambia. The court called for reparations, including safe return and restored property, and formally recognised the Rohingya as a distinct ethnic group. The ICJ cannot enforce its rulings, but they are binding. The decision is being watched as a precedent for South Africa's case against Israel over Gaza. Britain expelled Myanmar's defence attaché and asked for an urgent UN Security Council session, the EU said compliance is an obligation, and the US state department said it would study the ruling and warned against politicising the court. The Economist noted beforehand that the Rohingya heartland is now held by the Arakan Army, a rebel group also accused of abuses, which would complicate any return.

US district judge William Young in Boston ruled after a non-jury trial that the administration cannot bar providers funded by the Ryan White HIV/Aids Program from acknowledging transgender patients' gender identity or providing gender-affirming care. The conditions were added by the Health Resources and Services Administration from March to carry out Trump's executive order recognising only two sexes. In a 165-page ruling, the judge held that they punished doctors for their speech in breach of the First Amendment and discriminated on the basis of sex in breach of the Fifth. He wrote that they were rooted in "animus, loathing, and discrimination". The plaintiffs, who include the American Academy of HIV Medicine, argued that the conditions also conflicted with the programme's mandate, which has historically covered hormone therapy.

UK and EU near a post-Brexit reset deal

Al Jazeera · The Guardian

British and EU negotiators provisionally agreed on Thursday night to a youth mobility scheme allowing 100,000 EU citizens a year to live and work in the UK for up to three years, with the age limit raised from 30 to 31.

British and EU negotiators provisionally agreed on Thursday night to a youth mobility scheme allowing 100,000 EU citizens a year to live and work in the UK for up to three years, with the age limit raised from 30 to 31. Britain had originally sought 50,000 to 80,000, and at least as many young Britons will be able to go the other way. The EU dropped its last-minute demand that its students pay home tuition fees, leaving the details to a supplementary agreement within six months. On the food and drink deal, worth an estimated £5.1bn a year to Britain, the UK will follow EU rules on food and plant safety standards. It will be consulted on new laws but will not vote on them, after France resisted a bigger role. The three agreements to be signed at Friday's summit, including one on emissions trading, are expected to add 0.4% to UK GDP. Prime minister Andy Burnham said the test was whether the deals made life better in Britain. Reform UK's Nigel Farage called it a "betrayal".
